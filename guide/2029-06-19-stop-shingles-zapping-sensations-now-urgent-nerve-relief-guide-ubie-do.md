Explanation

Shingles (Herpes Zoster) is caused by the reactivation of the chickenpox virus (varicella-zoster) in nerve tissue. For many people, the rash and blistering are the most memorable parts of the illness. However, even after the rash heals, some experience lingering nerve pain. One common form of this pain is a sudden, electric shock–like sensation—often called "zapping" sensations or an electric shock feeling after shingles. Understanding why this happens and how to manage it can help you regain comfort and quality of life.

Why Do "Zapping" Sensations Occur?

When the varicella-zoster virus reactivates, it travels along the nerve fibers to the skin, causing inflammation and damage to nerves. After the rash clears, those same nerves may remain irritated or partly injured. The brain can misinterpret ordinary signals—or even lack of signals—as bursts of pain. This neuropathic pain can feel like:

Sharp, electric shocks

Burning or stabbing pains

Tingling or "pins and needles"

These sensations are most often part of a condition called postherpetic neuralgia (PHN). While PHN tends to occur in older adults, anyone who's had shingles can experience some form of persistent nerve pain.

Who Is at Risk?

Age over 50 (risk increases with age)

Severe shingles outbreak or a rash lasting more than 3–4 weeks

Shingles affecting the face or torso

Underlying conditions that weaken the immune system

Managing Electric Shock–Type Pain

No single treatment works for everyone. Often, a combination of approaches tailored to your pain level, other health conditions and personal preferences is most effective. Below are categories of strategies you and your healthcare provider might consider:

Medications

Anticonvulsants• Gabapentin (Neurontin)• Pregabalin (Lyrica)These drugs calm hyper-excitable nerves and are first-line treatments for PHN.

Antidepressants• Tricyclics (amitriptyline, nortriptyline)• SNRIs (duloxetine, venlafaxine)At low doses, they can modify pain signals in the spinal cord and brain.

Topical Agents• Lidocaine patches or creams• Capsaicin cream (0.025%–0.075%)Applied directly over the painful area, they provide localized relief with fewer systemic side effects.

Opioids (with caution)• Oxycodone, tramadolPrescribed only if other treatments fail, given the risk of tolerance, dependence and side effects.

Non-Drug Therapies

Transcutaneous Electrical Nerve Stimulation (TENS)Small electrical currents delivered through skin electrodes can interrupt pain signals.

Physical TherapyGentle stretching, strength exercises and massage may reduce stiffness and improve circulation around affected nerves.

AcupunctureInserting fine needles at specific points can stimulate natural pain-relieving chemicals in the body.

Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT) and MindfulnessTechniques to manage stress, anxiety and the emotional aspects of chronic pain.

Monitoring Progress and Adjusting Treatment

It's normal for nerve-related pains to wax and wane. Keep a simple pain diary noting:

Intensity (scale of 1–10)

Frequency and duration of zapping sensations

Triggers (temperature changes, stress, movement)

Relief measures that helped

Share this diary with your healthcare provider to fine-tune treatment. If you're not getting adequate relief after 4–6 weeks, ask about adjusting doses or trying a different combination of therapies.

When to Seek Medical Attention

Most zapping sensations after shingles improve with time and treatment. However, contact your doctor if you experience:

Worsening or very intense pain that interferes with daily life

New or spreading rash, especially around the eye area

Fever, chills or signs of infection at the old rash site

Numbness or weakness in the affected area

Any symptom that feels life-threatening or you cannot control
