# This Is What It's Really Like to Train Inside the NBA Bubble

The NBA season abruptly came to a halt on March 11 when the league made the decision to suspend play due to the rising prevalence of COVID-19 in the United States. Players went on lockdown, for the most part disconnecting from their respective training staffs and waiting for the call for basketball would resume. Then, in June, the league announced its plans to finish the season: 22 teams would be invited to Disney World in Orlando, FL to live and play inside a 'bubble'—an isolated community for only NBA players and staff.

Ahead of the return to play, we talked with Daniel Bove, the director of performance for the Phoenix Suns, to get a deeper look at what staying conditioned and game-ready looks like inside the NBA bubble. Bove, who was a strength and conditioning coach and applied sports scientist for the Atlanta Hawks before coming to Phoenix, shared how the Suns has adjusted training strategy to ensure a safe return-to-play once scrimmages begin July 22 and official games begin on July 30.

This interview has been edited and condensed for clarity.

When the season was put on hold, that coincided with a bunch of states shutting down. In that time, we were unable to do any type of training or see any of our players up-close-and-personal, so we immediately got into the mode of thinking about what kinds of workouts we can provide the players in their home. Some of them have home gyms, some of them don’t—I’d say it’s about half-and-half. It’s a lot easier to train the guys virtually who have a home gym as opposed to those who don’t.

Since our facilities weren’t being used at the time, we were able to deliver equipment to different players so they would have stuff to do. We would do things virtually and follow up with the players on a weekly or sometimes daily basis just to make sure they were all set. Our performance teams did a really good job of writing up programs at the very beginning of the pandemic so that the guys could actually follow and stay somewhat in shape.

A lot of what we did for them when they were in their homes was about protecting tendons and keeping joint health. So there are a couple of things we do, mainly isometric training, which is great for preserving that tendon health. That’s huge for us, especially considering that as soon as guys comes back, the number one risk is tendinopathy [a condition that causes pain in the tendons and affects range of motion]. So those were some things we aimed for when this all started. Because you’re in a team environment, when you take that team environment and everyone’s at home you don’t really know what to do. Everyone was craving [training]. We’re in a bubble now and we were starved of human interaction for four or five months. It’s actually kind of nice to be around the team again.

The NBA is building safety into teams' daily routines.

My first reaction [when the NBA announced the season would resume] was I was interested in hearing about the protocols in relation to cleanliness, the facilities. I understand being on the ground level how many layers there are to NBA basketball being played between what our equipment managers deal with on a daily basis to get guys what they need, the food, the travel, the bus schedules, the cleaning of the facilities, getting the arena time, getting the court time, for practices, for pre-game, for shoot-arounds. You really start to wonder, “Can 22 teams really make this happen in Disney World and then can they keep them safe?” So the first thing that came to mind was what are the protocols that are going to keep guys safe. The more we dug into it, the more it showed that the NBA had gone above and beyond making sure that the guys were safe.

[Our daily routine in the bubble] starts with waking up and breakfast is provided. Lunch and dinner are provided as well. Those are at set times. We have a practice schedule. I don’t know if this is official, but it’s my gut: The NBA makes the practice times for every team and those times change. So there might be one day where we practice from 6 p.m. to 9 p.m. and then the next day we might practice at 11 a.m. My guess is the NBA doesn’t want a competitive advantage for a team to have all morning practices or all night practices. My guess is they’re trying to flip-flop teams so everyone experiences practices at different times. So our practice schedule is all over the place.

What’s really interesting is that in this current time, we’re getting tested every single day. Every team has assigned slots where they’ll go down to the labs and everyone runs through and does their COVID test. Usually it’s around a meal time and it’s next to the meal room. We get our tests, get our food, go to practice, come back.

During the day when we’re not practicing, we provide time slots for guys to get training in. We actually traveled with some equipment. We have our own little conference room where we have a squat rack and our training boxes and all of our weights. Guys will come in and schedule times with us throughout the day. For practice, we have a 3-hour window. So you arrive, practice starts, and you have 3 hours. Within that 3 hours, we usually have a team lift as well. The way our team has been doing it is as soon as guys arrive for practice, we go to the weight room.

Our head strength coach, Cory, corrals everyone, gets them warmed up, we do a lift that’s 40 to 50 minutes long and then we get them right into basketball, so they have about 2 hours of basketball, and then we’re out. We’re very efficient with our time because we have a cleaning crew that comes in right after we’re done and they clean the weight room top to bottom, they clean the court top to bottom, and we’re out.

Training inside the bubble is different—but more similar to pre-COVID than you'd think.

We have every resource that we’d have available to us that we’d have at home. Partly that’s because we travel with a lot of equipment and technology. Being the director of performance, I oversee a lot of the testing so we travel with our force plate system. We get guys jumping almost daily.

I would say the biggest difference is the format of the workouts from a group standpoint. When we’re in Phoenix, we usually have a more individualized environment. You have one or two players training at a time and they get strong, individual attention. In the bubble, we’re still doing that as maintenance; guys will do that outside of practice time. But what’s really, really awesome and great for the team spirit is that in practice we get all 15 guys in the weight room and we lift as a team and build some camaraderie. It’s not that we didn’t have that before, but I think it’s a lot stronger here in the bubble. We wouldn’t do as many group workouts like that when we were in Phoenix.

The bubble is helping with players' focus.

I’ve noticed a huge difference in focus level from our team. I think it’s two things: One, these guys are in a bubble and they don’t have much else to focus on. They’re playing video games, they’re hanging out with teammates, they’re hanging out with staff, they’re walking around the hotel. But other than that, they don’t have a lot to do. So basketball and training are pretty much all they’re focusing on.

Number two, these guys have basically gone without basketball for four months. They’ve been starved of what they love, which I think has deepened their appreciation for what the sport’s offered them, not only as their jobs, but they just love playing. I see how excited they are. I know the first day that we started playing live basketball, where they were actually scrimmaging each other, it was some of the most intense, but also happy, basketball I’ve ever seen. So, no joke, it’s been a really cool thing to see the focus levels of the team.

But teams are still getting used to the bubble's many rules and regulations.

For our team, it’s more so guys figuring out where the boundaries are. We’ve never experienced an environment like this. I will say that you arrived at the hotel on Day One and we had to quarantine in our rooms for 48 hours. We weren’t allowed to leave at all for 48 hours, at all. You actually had to get two negative tests before you could come out of your room. So when we were in that time, we had pamphlets and care packages. A lot of the rules were explained on these pamphlets, but there were a lot [of rules]. I think still guys are trying to figure out what they can and cannot do. I think people are always going to want to push boundaries.

Once again, this is something no one has ever experienced, so I understand. I’ve been here for eight days and we can’t leave the hotel. It’s definitely an interesting place to be. On the 22nd when scrimmages start, we’re allowed to visit other team hotels. I’m in a hotel with six teams and there are other hotels, and I have colleagues that I worked with from other teams. You can go visit them, play golf. So on the 22nd, we’ll be able to do all that.

There's a major emphasis on injury prevention.

We’re on pins and needles wondering if we prepared these guys enough coming into the bubble. There were a couple weeks where we were able to train the guys at our Phoenix facility. With social distancing, we could only have so many athletes at a time, so there were definitely parameters that were tight. But since we couldn’t play the game of basketball leading up to the bubble, it’s very difficult to train and prepare them for the sport from an injury protection standpoint.

The number one priority for us was to make sure we were still loading tissues and tendons from a structural standpoint. Things like tendinopathies and soft tissue injuries, hamstring, hip flexors, quads, whatever. All those prevalent in a sport like basketball. So we wanted to make sure we were preparing the lower weight structure for the lows they’re going to hit when they start playing again. I spoke about it earlier, but things like isometrics are huge for tendon health. Our guys do that almost daily.

And then from a force perspective, sprinting. A lot of the research shows that maximal velocity sprinting is one of the best ways to prevent people from soft tissue injuries in the lower body. Three to four times a week, although guys were only able to work on it individually, we would make sure that max velocity sprinting and decelerations on the court would prep them for how they were going to hit when we got to the bubble. That in addition to three or four conditioning sessions a week that we tried to implement when we were in Phoenix.

You can never mimic 5-on-5 basketball, so you just try and do your best to make sure they’re prepped and ready to go so when you ramp it up it’s not too much a shock to the system. Then when it comes to starting the team workouts in the bubble, when we were finally able to play real basketball, it’s about managing workloads in a smart way so moving forward we’re making sure that you’re not jumping more than 20% in workloads per week. You want to make sure you’re doing gradual increases to the best of your abilities. It’s not necessarily how much work you do in a week that’s always problematic. Sometimes it’s how fast you got to that load. Week One, Week Two, Week Three, you don’t want the slope of increase to be that quick. So we do our best to monitor that way.

Staff members are getting creative to work out inside the bubble.

Every facility that we go to for practice, they have a full-on weight room. There are seven practice facilities and each one of the practice facilities has a brand-new weight room. Only the players can use those facilities, so when we’re at the hotel, we have to get really creative with our staff coming up with workouts outside and trying to keep distance from each other. Staff members are also trying to stay fit and sane in the bubble because we don’t get to play basketball every day and we don’t get to lift in the weight rooms every day, so we’ve had to get creative at the hotel.
