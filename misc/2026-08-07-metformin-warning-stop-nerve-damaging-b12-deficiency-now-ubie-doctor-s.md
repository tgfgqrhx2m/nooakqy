Why Long-Term Metformin Use May Cause Nerve-Damaging B12 Deficiency

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Long-term metformin use may reduce vitamin B12 absorption by speeding gut transit, disrupting intestinal receptors, promoting bacterial overgrowth, and interacting with dietary intake. Left unaddressed, this deficiency can damage nerves and cause lasting neurological problems.

Early warning signs of metformin-induced B12 deficiency include:

Tingling in the hands and feet

Numbness or reduced sensation

Balance and coordination problems

Persistent fatigue

Cognitive changes or memory issues

Screening, prevention, and treatment options vary based on your health history, symptoms, and how long you've been on metformin — making it important to understand your individual risk before deciding on next steps.

Explanation

Metformin is one of the most commonly prescribed medications for type 2 diabetes. It helps lower blood sugar, improves insulin sensitivity, and has a strong safety record. However, long-term metformin use has been linked to decreased vitamin B12 absorption. Left unchecked, this can lead to nerve-damaging B12 deficiency. Understanding why this happens, recognizing symptoms, and taking simple steps to prevent complications can keep you healthy and feeling your best.

How Metformin Affects Vitamin B12 Absorption

Metformin and B12 deficiency are connected through several biological processes:

• Altered gut motility – Metformin can change how quickly food moves through your digestive tract. – Faster transit time may limit the opportunity for B12 to bind to intrinsic factor, a protein essential for absorption.

• Changes in intestinal cells – Metformin may interfere with calcium-dependent receptors in the small intestine that help uptake the B12–intrinsic factor complex. – Reduced receptor efficiency lowers the amount of B12 entering the bloodstream.

• Small bowel bacterial overgrowth – Metformin can sometimes promote bacterial overgrowth in the small intestine. – Certain bacteria consume B12, further reducing your available stores.

• Dietary factors – Many people on long-term metformin may not consume enough B12-rich foods (meat, fish, dairy, fortified cereals). – Even with a balanced diet, impaired absorption can lead to deficiency.

How Common Is Metformin-Induced B12 Deficiency?

Studies show that up to 30% of people taking metformin for more than four years have lower-than-normal B12 levels. Of these, roughly 10–20% develop clinically significant deficiency. The risk increases with:

• Higher doses of metformin (>1,000 mg/day)• Longer duration of therapy (>5 years)• Older age (especially over 60)• Vegetarian or vegan diets• Pre-existing gastrointestinal issues (e.g., Crohn's disease, celiac disease)

Why Vitamin B12 Matters for Nerve Health

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is crucial for:

• Myelin sheath formation – Myelin covers and insulates nerves, ensuring rapid electrical signaling. – Without enough B12, myelin breaks down, leading to nerve irritation and damage.

• Red blood cell production – B12 is needed to form healthy red blood cells. – Deficiency can cause anemia, leading to fatigue and weakness.

• DNA synthesis and repair – B12 plays a role in making and maintaining genetic material. – Low levels can impair cell division and overall tissue health.

Symptoms of B12 Deficiency on Nerves

Early B12 deficiency may be subtle. Over time, nerve damage can become serious. Watch for:

• Tingling or "pins and needles" in hands and feet (paresthesia)• Numbness or decreased sensation in extremities• Unsteady walking or balance problems• Muscle weakness or cramps• Memory lapses, confusion, or mood changes• Fatigue, shortness of breath (from anemia)• Glossitis (inflamed tongue) or mouth ulcers

Diagnosing B12 Deficiency

Your doctor can confirm B12 status through:

• Serum vitamin B12 level• Methylmalonic acid (MMA) test (elevated in true B12 deficiency)• Homocysteine level (can rise when B12 is low)• Complete blood count (CBC) looking for anemia or abnormal red cells• Neurological exam to assess reflexes, sensation, and gait

Early diagnosis is vital. Nerve damage can be reversible if treated promptly, but prolonged deficiency may lead to permanent changes.

Preventing Nerve-Damaging B12 Deficiency

If you're taking metformin, you don't have to wait for symptoms. Consider these steps:

• Routine screening – Ask your doctor to check B12 levels annually if you've been on metformin for over two years.

• Dietary adjustments – Include B12-rich foods: • Lean meats (beef, poultry) • Fish (salmon, tuna) • Eggs and dairy (milk, yogurt, cheese) • Fortified cereals and plant-based milks

• Oral supplements – Over-the-counter B12 tablets or sublingual lozenges can help maintain levels. – Typical preventive dose: 250–1,000 mcg daily.

• Prescription injections – For those with significant malabsorption, monthly intramuscular B12 shots may be recommended. – Injection regimens vary; follow your doctor's advice.

• Calcium supplementation – Some studies suggest adding 1,200 mg of elemental calcium daily can improve B12 absorption when taken with metformin. – Check with your doctor before starting any new supplement.

What to Do If You Have Symptoms

Don't panic. Many people on metformin develop mild B12 deficiency that's easy to treat.

Track your symptoms. Note when tingling, fatigue, or memory changes began and how they progress.

Discuss test results and treatment options with your healthcare provider.

Follow through with recommended supplements or injections as prescribed.

Reassess your B12 levels every 6–12 months until stable.

Balancing Metformin's Benefits and B12 Health

Metformin remains a cornerstone of type 2 diabetes management. Its benefits include:

• Lowering A1C levels by 1–2%• Weight neutrality or modest weight loss• Low risk of hypoglycemia when used alone• Possible cardiovascular protection

Managing the risk of B12 deficiency ensures you continue to enjoy these benefits without compromising nerve health.

When to Seek Medical Attention Immediately

While mild B12 deficiency can be corrected without severe consequences, some signs require urgent care:

• Sudden, severe numbness or paralysis• Confusion, hallucinations, or significant cognitive decline• Shortness of breath at rest, chest pain, or rapid heart rate (signs of severe anemia)• Loss of bowel or bladder control (possible spinal cord involvement)
