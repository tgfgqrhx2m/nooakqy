# The Secret Offseason Camp Where NFL Linemen Get in Shape

The workout opens with a drumbeat of thuds. D. J. Humphries, 26, six-five, 301 pounds of brawn, staggers his feet, places his hands in front of his chest, and braces. He’s prepping for a simulated bull rush. Justin Pugh, 30, six-four, 311 pounds of muscle, holds a hand bar with four orange balls on it and punches toward Humphries, who parries each hit with a thwack. It’s kinda like sumo as each giant tries to hold his ground.

The drill kicks off an hour-long session for Humphries and Pugh, teammates on the Arizona Cardinals’ O-line, doing everything from sprints to 400-pound deadlifts to pushing 150-pound sleds with so much oomph that the sleds fly off the ground. It ends with both men walking over to a set of pullup bars. As Future’s “Too Comfortable” blares on the loudspeakers, Humphries hangs from a bar, then pulls his chest toward it. Pugh keeps count—and marvels. “You ever see a 300-pound man do 15 pull-ups in a row?” he asks.

Hang out at Offensive Line Performance (OLP for short) and you witness big men showcasing stunning athleticism. Founded by ex-NFLer LeCharles Bentley, 40, the 20,000-square-foot facility caters to a very specific clientele: what NFL general managers call “hog mollies” or “big uglies.” Only offensive linemen—the behemoths who protect the likes of Brady and Mahomes and clear out lanes for Zeke Elliot and Saquon Barkley—can train here.

Every spring, when the NFL’s biggest names head to off-season facilities in Florida and California to run routes and hone their strength, the NFL’s biggest men descend on suburban Chandler, Arizona, to train in a space that includes a turf field and a massive weight room. These guys arrive with many of the same gym goals you have: They want to drop a few pounds, add a little muscle, move a bit faster. “These were the kids who sat by themselves at the lunch table and were picked on,” says Bentley. “This is the everyday person. When you pull back the curtain, they’re dealing with the same issues as the 80 percent of our country that’s obese. But they figure out how to work through it.”

That was Bentley’s goal when he retired in 2007. A two-time Pro Bowl center who broke into the NFL in 2002, he’d spent his off-seasons working out alongside quarterbacks and running backs, doing workouts hardly tailored to his lumbering 309-pound frame. Linemen needed routines built for them, and when his own career was cut short by injury, Bentley resolved to fill the void himself. “It’s all about empowering people,” he says. Bentley studied everything from basic strength training to nutrition to functional movement and fascial stretching, and by 2008 he’d founded OLP as a small camp in Ohio, working with a handful of young linemen. Five years later, he moved operations to Chandler.

The invite-only off-season training program, open to just 30 participants at a time, delivers both individually tailored workouts and unique camaraderie. Each morning, players arrive in pairs, as Humphries and Pugh did at noon, and as free agent Kelvin Beachum, 31, six-three, 305 pounds, and Packers rookie Jake Hanson, 23, six-four, 309 pounds, did before that. There they meet a trainer, who pushes them through a workout created by Bentley. Training with a partner ensures that every lineman competes with a peer, and keeps morale high.

Most linemen have spent their entire career absorbing big-man stereotypes, and yes, it gets in their head. Beachum says his wife jokes with him that she’ll leave him if “I get fat when I’m done playing.” Detroit Lions tackle Taylor Decker, 27, six-seven, 318 pounds, obsesses over what he terms “productive paranoia,” fearing that if he rests too long, he’ll fall too far out of shape to get ready for the season. Giants rookie Shane Lemieux , 23, six-three, 311 pounds, arrived at Bentley’s rookie program in January and avoided taking shirtless pictures. “I looked like a truck driver,” he says. Three months later, Lemieux’s body fat was down ten points (from 31 percent to 21 percent). “It’s better to be lean,” he says. “You can do it all when you’re lean.” And you can get lean, build strength, and add athleticism if you follow the training rules from the NFL’s largest athletes.

RULE 1: LUNGE TOWARD ATHLETICISM

WHETHER YOU'RE leaping for a layup on the basketball court or chasing your son in the backyard, most athletic moves start from what’s called an offset stance, one leg behind the other, front foot flat on the floor, back heel off the floor. It’s critical for offensive linemen. For Beachum, when the ball is snapped, he must explode either backward (to protect his quarterback) or forward (to block for his running back). That means explosive single-leg strength. And no move builds that better than the classic barbell lunge. “We want that initial drive to be as powerful as possible so he can cover ground efficiently,” Bentley says.

Your Move: At least twice a week, do 4 sets of 5 to 8 reps of lunges per leg. Rest 90 seconds between each set. On each rep, start standing, then step forward and bend your front knee, lowering your front thigh until it’s parallel to the floor. Bend your back knee as you do this, but don’t let it crash into the floor. Reverse the movement to stand up. Add challenge by holding dumbbells at your sides.

Don’t think of the lunge as a secondary leg exercise, either, says Beachum. When done correctly, it’s a perfect alternative to heavy squats and other body-battering leg moves. “As I get older, I can’t squat, power-clean, and hang-snatch all day,” he says. “I get the biggest gains out of lunges.”

RULE 2: CRAWL BEFORE YOU WALK

IT'S NOT all heavy weights at OLP. Sometimes it’s challenging to manage your own bodyweight. That’s what Cincinnati Bengals guard Xavier Su’a-Filo learned when he arrived this off-season to work his way back from the fractured left fibula and torn deltoid ligament that wrecked his 2019 season.

He knew he’d need to regain foot and ankle strength. But Bentley asked the six-four, 304-pound Su’a-Filo to get on his hands and knees and crawl like a bear. Sounds easy enough. “These crawls wipe you out,” the 29-year-old says. They also strengthened Su’a-Filo’s core and added stability to his shoulders while teaching him to properly push off his injured right ankle.

Even better: Unlike with, say, heavy power cleans or squats, Su’a-Filo doesn’t need days to recover, so he can train more frequently. “There are other ways to challenge the rest of your body and be low-impact,” he says.

Your Move: Start every workout with bear crawls. This will blast your abs, and it’ll improve ankle strength and mobility if you focus on keeping your shins parallel to the floor.

“It reconnects you to basic athleticism,” Bentley says. “A good athlete is going to be able to connect the upper and lower body.” Start with 4 sets, covering 20 to 25 yards each, and work up to 6 sets. Rest 90 seconds between each.

RULE 3: Build Posture, Romanian-Style

THE MINNESOTA VIKINGS' Pat Elflein loves a good leg workout, since it translates directly to the field. “You play football with your legs,” he says. “Leg day is the single most important day of the week.” That’s not just because it blasts his quads and hamstrings, either. His favorite move, the Romanian deadlift, also hones his posture. And that, says Bentley, may be the most critical thing for Elflein, 26, a six-four, 295-pound guard. “You’re dealing with an opponent who is trying to break your posture every snap,” says Bentley.

The single-leg Romanian deadlift is generally viewed as a hamstring and glute move, but it challenges your upper body, too, pushing you to keep your abs and shoulder blades tight as you lower your torso toward the ground. “If you train an athlete to establish proper posture,” says Bentley, “they connect to it, and it becomes easier to build technique.”

Your Move: Add single-leg RDLs to your training at least once a week. To do one, hold dumbbells at your hips and tighten your core. Lift your left foot off the floor and push your butt backward as you lower your torso to the floor. Pause when you start to feel your back rounding, or when your torso is parallel to the floor, whichever comes first, then stand back up. Do 5 sets of 6 reps per side.

Rule 4: Lose Your Shoes

THE CLASSIC deadlift is the king of total-body exercises, building glute and hamstring power and core strength, too. But it can also enhance foot and toe strength and mobility. That matters to Decker, because better foot strength helps him move more quickly to protect his quarterback—and it can help you move more quickly, whether you’re running or playing with your dog.

“If you don’t have the flexibility you need, you’re not starting in the proper stance,” says Decker. The deadlift becomes a toe builder when you take your shoes off to do it. Doing so forces you to grip the floor more aggressively with your toes, creating pressure as you flex them, while stretching the fascia that runs through your foot. Decker also keeps training those foot muscles away from his deadlifts, doing other exercises barefoot and wearing yoga stretchers at home and in the locker room. “Anything I can do without shoes, I like it way, way better,” he says.

Your Move: Aim to deadlift twice a week. Do one of those sessions barefoot. Do 4 sets of 5 reps either way, and own the basics: Your hips must stay below your shoulders, and you should squeeze your abs and shoulder blades before every rep.

Focus on your big toe, says Bentley, flexing it hard to “grip” the floor. “When you’re driving into the deadlift, you’re driving the big toes into the ground,” he says. “Early on, people just crack. You’re contracting motions that you haven’t contracted before.”

Rule 5: Step Up to the Pullup

THE PULLUP is a gold-standard bodyweight move, but you won’t see offensive linemen doing it during NFL training camps. Why? The answer has its roots in grade-school gym class. “It’s because it was always okay for the fat kid to not be able to do them,” says Bentley.

Not at OLP. Yes, just about every offensive lineman would rather bench-press or deadlift heavy weights than hang from a bar and haul his chest high into the air, but Bentley doesn’t care. Pullups are on the workout menu a few times a week. “It’s a true example of functional strength,” says Bentley. “If you don’t add that to your toolbox, you’re limiting yourself as an athlete. When you get to the point where you can do a pullup, you’ve shown that your body-fat/body-mass ratio is more in line with where it needs to be.”

Pulling off the pullup also means you’ve built strong lats. That lat strength helps linemen absorb and redirect incoming pass rushers on the field. Off the field, it can help safeguard your shoulders.

Your Move: Inject pullups into your training at least twice a week, chasing 2 to 3 sets of 4 to 5 reps. That worked for Beachum, who couldn’t do a single rep when he started working out at OLP in 2013. Now he does 2 to 3 sets of 8 to 10 reps every other day. Can’t do a single rep? Start with eccentric reps: Jump up to the bar and slowly lower yourself to the ground. Do 2 sets of 6 reps.
