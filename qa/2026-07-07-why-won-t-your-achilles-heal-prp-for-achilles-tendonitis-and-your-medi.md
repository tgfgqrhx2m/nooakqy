Why Your Achilles Isn't Healing and the Role of PRP for Achilles Tendonitis

Achilles tendonitis can derail your workouts, slow you down on the track, or keep you off your feet entirely. If rest, ice and stretching haven't done the trick, you may wonder why your Achilles still hurts—and whether advanced treatments like PRP for Achilles tendonitis are worth considering. This guide breaks down common reasons for stalled healing, explains how PRP works, and lays out sensible next steps.

Why Achilles Tendonitis Can Become Chronic

Poor Blood Supply

The mid-portion of the Achilles tendon has limited blood flow.

Less blood means fewer nutrients and healing cells reach the damaged area.

Overuse and Biomechanics

Repetitive stress (running, jumping) can outpace your body's repair.

Flat feet, tight calves, or high arches alter loading on the tendon.

Inadequate Rest or Too Much Rest

Pushing through pain delays healing.

Conversely, total immobilization weakens the tendon and calf muscles.

Muscle Imbalances

Tight or weak calf muscles place extra load on the tendon.

Hip or core weakness can also change your gait and strain your Achilles.

Age and Tissue Quality

Tendons become stiffer and less elastic with age (especially after 35).

Healing slows down, and microtears accumulate over time.

Misdiagnosis or Overlooked Problems

Posterior ankle impingement, bursitis or partial tears can mimic tendonitis.

Imaging (ultrasound or MRI) may be needed to confirm the exact issue.

Standard Treatments Before Considering PRP

Before exploring platelet-rich plasma (PRP), most physicians recommend:

Relative RestReduce high-impact activities but keep gentle movement.

Ice and HeatIce after activity to reduce inflammation; heat before workouts to loosen tissue.

Nonsteroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs)Short-term use can ease pain. Long-term use may interfere with tendon repair.

What Is PRP for Achilles Tendonitis?

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is an injection-based therapy that uses your own blood components to promote healing:

Blood Drawn from Your ArmA small sample (15–60 mL) is taken, similar to a routine blood test.

Centrifuge SeparationThe sample spins at high speed to concentrate platelets into a small volume of plasma.

Platelet-Rich Plasma CollectedPlatelets contain growth factors and signaling proteins that may aid tissue repair.

Ultrasound-Guided InjectionYour doctor precisely injects PRP into the damaged portion of the Achilles tendon.

Key benefits of PRP:

Uses your own blood—minimal risk of allergic reaction

Can potentially accelerate healing in low-blood-flow areas

May reduce pain and improve function without steroids

Limitations to keep in mind:

Results vary; not everyone responds the same way

Typically requires 1–3 injections spaced weeks apart

Out-of-pocket costs can be high (often $500–$2,000 per injection)

Who Is a Good Candidate for PRP?

Ideal candidates for PRP for Achilles tendonitis:

Persistent pain despite 3–6 months of conservative care

Moderate to severe tendon degeneration on imaging (ultrasound or MRI)

No active infection or bleeding disorder

Realistic expectations about time frame and possible need for additional treatments

Willingness to follow post-injection rehabilitation

Not recommended if you have:

Complete Achilles rupture (usually requires surgery)

Active skin infection at the injection site

Severe systemic illness or uncontrolled diabetes

Platelet disorders or are on certain blood thinners

Potential Risks and Side Effects

PRP is generally safe, but you should be aware of possible complications:

Temporary increase in pain and swelling at the injection site (1–3 days)

Infection (rare if proper sterile technique is used)

No improvement or incomplete healing

Bleeding or bruising in patients on blood thinners

Other Advanced or Alternative Treatments

If PRP is not suitable or you want to explore all options, consider:

Extracorporeal Shockwave Therapy (ESWT)Sound waves target the tendon to stimulate healing.

ProlotherapyInjection of dextrose solution to trigger a mild inflammatory response.

Topical Nitric Oxide PatchesMay increase blood flow and aid collagen production.

Surgical OptionsDebridement or tendon augmentation for severe, chronic cases.

Biologics and Stem CellsEmerging treatments still under clinical study.

When to Re-Evaluate or Seek Further Care

No improvement after 6–12 months of combined conservative and PRP treatment

Increasing pain, redness or warmth (signs of infection)

New weakness or sudden inability to bear weight (possible tendon tear)

Concerns about underlying conditions (e.g., arthritis, nerve entrapment)

Take-Home Message and Next Steps

Chronic Achilles tendonitis often reflects a mix of poor blood flow, overuse, biomechanics and age-related tissue changes.

Standard care—rest, ice, NSAIDs, eccentric exercises and physical therapy—remains the foundation.

PRP for Achilles tendonitis can be a useful next step: it harnesses your own growth factors to potentially boost healing.

Results are variable; discuss candidly with your doctor about costs, risks and realistic expectations.

If PRP isn't right for you, other options include shockwave therapy, prolotherapy or, in severe cases, surgery.

Always speak to a qualified healthcare provider about any persistent or worsening symptoms. If you experience sudden severe pain, inability to walk, or signs of infection, seek medical attention promptly—these issues can be serious and may require immediate care.
