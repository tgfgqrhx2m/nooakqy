High Neutrophils? Why Your Blood Count Is Elevated & Medically Approved Next Steps

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

High neutrophils on a complete blood count (CBC) usually reflect a temporary response to infection, inflammation, stress, certain medications, or smoking. In rare cases, elevated neutrophils may signal a bone marrow disorder. The severity of the count and whether it persists over time help determine how concerning it is.

Recommended next steps include reviewing your symptoms and current medications, repeating the CBC, and running targeted tests for infection or inflammation. A specialist evaluation may be needed if counts remain very high. Seek urgent care immediately for red flags such as high fever, chest pain, severe shortness of breath, or confusion.

Explanation

If your lab report says neutrophils high, it's normal to feel concerned. Neutrophils are a type of white blood cell that help your body fight infection. When levels rise above the normal range, it's called neutrophilia.

In many cases, high neutrophils are a temporary and expected response to stress, illness, or inflammation. Sometimes, however, they can signal a more serious underlying condition.

This guide explains what it means when neutrophils are high, common causes, and what medically appropriate next steps look like.

What Are Neutrophils?

Neutrophils are the most abundant type of white blood cell. They:

Fight bacteria and some fungi

Respond quickly to infections

Help control inflammation

Remove damaged tissue

They are measured as part of a complete blood count (CBC) with differential.

Normal Range

Normal neutrophil levels vary slightly by lab, but generally:

40–60% of total white blood cells

Absolute neutrophil count (ANC): 1,500–8,000 cells per microliter

When levels rise above this range, your report may indicate neutrophils high or elevated ANC.

What Causes High Neutrophils?

Neutrophilia usually means your immune system is activated. The reason can be mild and temporary — or more complex.

1. Infection (Most Common Cause)

The most frequent reason for high neutrophils is a bacterial infection.

Examples include:

Pneumonia

Urinary tract infection (UTI)

Skin infections

Appendicitis

2. Inflammation

Any condition that causes inflammation may increase neutrophils, including:

Rheumatoid arthritis

Inflammatory bowel disease

Vasculitis

Tissue injury or trauma

Post-surgical healing

Inflammation signals the immune system to produce more white blood cells.

6. Bone Marrow Conditions (Less Common but Serious)

In rare cases, persistently high neutrophils may point to a bone marrow disorder, such as:

Chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML)

Other myeloproliferative disorders

These conditions usually cause additional abnormalities on a blood test — not just high neutrophils alone.

How High Is "Too High"?

The degree of elevation matters.

Mild elevation: Often linked to minor infection or stress

Moderate elevation: Suggests active infection or inflammation

Severely high counts (very high ANC): May require urgent evaluation

When Should You Be Concerned?

High neutrophils deserve attention if:

The elevation is severe

It persists over multiple tests

You have unexplained fever

You have night sweats or weight loss

You feel progressively worse

Other blood counts are abnormal

Seek urgent medical care if you experience:

High fever (over 103°F / 39.4°C)

Chest pain

Severe shortness of breath

Confusion

Signs of sepsis (rapid heart rate, low blood pressure)

These can be life-threatening situations and require immediate evaluation.

Medically Approved Next Steps

If your report shows neutrophils high, here's what doctors typically recommend:

1. Review Your Symptoms

Your healthcare provider will ask:

Do you have signs of infection?

Any recent illness or surgery?

New medications?

Recent stress or injury?

Context is critical.

2. Repeat the Blood Test

Sometimes levels normalize on repeat testing.

A follow-up CBC can determine:

If the elevation was temporary

If levels are rising or stable

If other blood cells are affected

3. Check for Infection

Depending on symptoms, your doctor may order:

Urine tests

Chest X-ray

Blood cultures

Inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR)

Treatment may involve antibiotics if a bacterial infection is confirmed.

4. Review Medications

Your provider may adjust medications known to increase neutrophils.

Never stop a prescribed medication without medical guidance.

5. Additional Testing (If Needed)

If neutrophils remain persistently high without a clear cause, further testing might include:

Peripheral blood smear

Bone marrow testing (in rare cases)

Genetic testing (if leukemia is suspected)

These steps are not common — they are reserved for specific situations.

Can You Lower High Neutrophils Naturally?

Neutrophils are elevated because of an underlying issue. The goal is not to lower the number directly, but to address the cause.

Helpful steps include:

Treat infections promptly

Manage chronic inflammatory conditions

Stop smoking

Reduce unmanaged stress

Follow your doctor's treatment plan

Once the underlying issue resolves, neutrophil levels often return to normal.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are high neutrophils always serious?

No. Most cases are related to infection or stress and resolve with treatment.

Can dehydration cause high neutrophils?

Severe dehydration can temporarily concentrate blood components, but it's not a common cause of significant neutrophilia.

How long do neutrophils stay elevated?

It depends on the cause. With infection, they often normalize within days to weeks after recovery.

The Bottom Line

Seeing neutrophils high on a blood test can be unsettling, but it's often a sign your immune system is doing its job.

Common causes include:

Bacterial infections

Inflammation

Stress

Medications

Smoking

Less commonly, it may signal a bone marrow disorder.

The most important step is not to panic — but not to ignore it either.

What You Should Do Now

Review your symptoms

Schedule follow-up testing if recommended

Monitor for worsening symptoms

Speak to a doctor about any persistent, severe, or unexplained changes

If you experience high fever, chest pain, confusion, or other severe symptoms, seek emergency medical care immediately.

Blood tests are tools — they provide clues, not final answers. A qualified healthcare professional can interpret your results in the full context of your health.

When in doubt, speak to a doctor. It's the safest and most responsible next step.
