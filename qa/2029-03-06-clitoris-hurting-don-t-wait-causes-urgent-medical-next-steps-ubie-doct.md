Persistent Pain? Why Your Clitoris Is Hurting & Medical Next Steps

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Persistent clitoral pain can result from irritation, infections (yeast or STIs), clitoral adhesions, nerve or hormonal changes, skin conditions, or injury. Cancer is rare but important to rule out.

Start with gentle care: use fragrance-free products and reduce friction. Seek medical evaluation if pain lasts more than a few days, is severe, or is accompanied by sores, lumps, swelling, fever, or bleeding.

Because clitoral pain has many possible causes—ranging from easily treated irritation to conditions needing prompt care—identifying the likely source is the fastest way to relief. Explanation

Persistent pain in the clitoris can feel alarming, frustrating, and confusing. Because the clitoris contains thousands of nerve endings, it is one of the most sensitive parts of the body. Even minor irritation can feel intense.

While many causes of clitoral pain are not dangerous, ongoing or worsening symptoms should never be ignored. Understanding what may be happening — and knowing when to seek medical care — can help you take control of your health.

First: What's Normal — and What's Not?

The clitoris is highly sensitive to touch, pressure, and hormonal changes. Temporary soreness can happen after:

Sexual activity

Prolonged friction (tight clothing, cycling)

Masturbation

Use of new soaps, lubricants, or hygiene products

Mild sensitivity that improves within a day or two is usually not concerning.

However, persistent pain, burning, throbbing, swelling, or sharp sensations that last more than a few days deserve attention.

Common Causes of Clitoral Pain

1. Irritation or Contact Dermatitis

One of the most common reasons for clitoral pain is simple irritation.

Triggers may include:

Scented soaps or body washes

Bubble baths

Feminine hygiene sprays

Laundry detergents

Tight synthetic underwear

Pads or panty liners

The skin of the clitoris and vulva is delicate. Even products labeled "gentle" can disrupt the skin barrier.

Symptoms may include:

Burning

Itching

Redness

Raw or tender feeling

Switching to fragrance-free products and wearing breathable cotton underwear often helps.

2. Yeast Infections

A vaginal yeast infection can also affect the clitoris.

Common signs:

Intense itching

Thick white discharge

Redness and swelling

Burning during urination

The clitoris may feel extra sensitive or painful because of surrounding inflammation.

Over-the-counter antifungal treatments can help, but if symptoms persist, see a doctor to confirm the diagnosis.

3. Bacterial Infections or STIs

Certain infections can cause clitoral pain, including:

Herpes (often causes painful blisters or sores)

Bacterial vaginosis (less commonly painful but can irritate)

Other sexually transmitted infections (STIs)

Herpes in particular may cause:

Sharp, burning pain

Tingling before sores appear

Visible blisters or ulcers

Any new sores, lesions, or flu-like symptoms should prompt medical evaluation.

4. Clitoral Adhesions

Clitoral adhesions occur when the clitoral hood sticks to the clitoris. This can trap debris (called smegma), causing:

Sharp pain

Hypersensitivity

Discomfort with arousal

Difficulty retracting the hood

5. Nerve Pain (Clitorodynia)

Because the clitoris contains a high concentration of nerve endings, nerve-related pain can occur even without visible changes.

This may feel like:

Electric or stabbing sensations

Ongoing hypersensitivity

Pain without obvious cause

Clitorodynia (pain specifically in the clitoris) may be related to:

Pelvic floor dysfunction

Pudendal neuralgia

Past injury or trauma

Chronic vulvar pain conditions

Nerve pain requires medical evaluation and may benefit from pelvic floor therapy or medication.

6. Hormonal Changes

Low estrogen levels — especially during:

Menopause

Breastfeeding

Postpartum recovery

Certain hormonal treatments

— can cause thinning and dryness of vulvar tissue.

This may lead to:

Increased friction pain

Tenderness

Micro-tears

Heightened clitoral sensitivity

Topical estrogen (prescribed by a doctor) often helps when hormones are the cause.

7. Skin Conditions

Certain dermatologic conditions affect the vulva and clitoris, including:

Lichen sclerosus

Lichen planus

Psoriasis

Eczema

These may cause:

White patches

Skin thinning

Itching and burning

Pain with touch

These conditions require medical diagnosis and prescription treatment. Left untreated, some can cause scarring.

8. Trauma or Injury

Clitoral pain may follow:

Aggressive sexual activity

Accidental injury

Piercings

Surgical procedures

If pain follows a clear injury and improves steadily, it may simply need time. If it worsens, spreads, or includes swelling or discharge, seek care.

What to Expect at a Medical Appointment

If you see a doctor about clitoral pain, they may:

Ask about symptom timing and triggers

Review sexual and medical history

Perform a gentle external exam

Swab for infection if needed

Evaluate for skin conditions

Assess pelvic floor tension

In some cases, referral to a gynecologist, dermatologist, or pelvic floor physical therapist may be recommended.

Remember: healthcare providers are trained to handle these concerns professionally. You are not overreacting by seeking care.

Practical Steps You Can Take Now

While waiting for evaluation, consider:

Switching to fragrance-free soap (or just warm water)

Wearing loose, cotton underwear

Avoiding tight leggings or synthetic fabrics

Skipping scented pads or liners

Pausing sexual activity if it worsens pain

Avoiding douching (never recommended)

Do not self-treat repeatedly without knowing the cause. For example, frequent antifungal use without a confirmed yeast infection can worsen irritation.
